The pivot

From managing to building.

The next value creation was in building — and the construction supply chain was full of stacked markups we could flatten.

  • Pulled trades out of our own management business — electricians, plumbers — onto our builds
  • Removed layers of subcontractor markup
  • Took our best carpenters and pointed them at our first ground-up build

Then the framers walked on day one — they didn't understand what they'd quoted. So we brought framing in-house too. The pattern: every time the market failed us, we absorbed the capability.

A note on our budgets

The overages are the point.

Our budgets were never forecasts. They were deliberately aggressive lines in the sand — a number we didn't yet know how to hit, set to force every cost-out decision.

So yes — we came in over "budget," by design. The gap is the learning, not a miss. Each overage told us exactly where the next dollar comes out.

Design for cost

Engineering the waste out.

Most of a build's cost is convention, not necessity. So we questioned every component.

  • Limited the number of seams
  • Reduced outlet count to what's actually needed
  • Optimized trusses for usable storage space
  • Stripped out every non-essential component
  • Advanced framing: 2×6 at 24" on-center with a double sill plate

The cost-out case study

The wall decision.

We tested three ways to build exterior walls. The answer reframed the whole business.

Ware Butler prefab

$13K
Cheapest sticker price
  • Uninsulated
  • Still must insulate + finish on site

Frame our own

~$30K
Most labor, most schedule risk
  • 4 weeks of labor
  • + $10K in materials
  • Insulated on site
The winner

EcoPanels

$17K
Near-turnkey off the truck
  • R-26 insulated
  • Pre-sided with LP SmartSide
  • Wiring/plumbing chases pre-cut
  • ~1 week setup
The insulation is the same tech as a Yeti cooler — a continuous foam core that holds R-26.
No thermal bridging through studs. The panel arrives pre-sided with chases already cut — you set it and move on. This is the moment the "kit of parts" idea became obvious.
Projecting ≈ $20K for exterior walls going forward (preliminary — still firming up) — buying down schedule risk and labor exposure, not just material cost.
And then the calendar fought us

Control the schedule, or it controls your capital.

The town pushed our start date by a month — so we missed the window to get slabs in before winter, and carried the land through the cold months on Brett's own cash.

That lesson is now baked into the model. Schedule certainty isn't a nicety; it's the difference between a build that pencils and one that bleeds.

The product insight

One chassis. Many homes.

A single 1,500 sqft chassis — standard supplies and standard build procedures — reconfigured into four products. Same parts, same crew muscle memory; different home. The cost-out and schedule certainty we learned the hard way compounds across every configuration.

The thesis
